# Collage architectures

Choose one architecture from source geometry. These are construction systems, not fixed templates.

## Edge-borne anchor

Place the photographic hero against one canvas edge so it enters the artwork at full visual weight. Convert the wider setting into an illustration field that continues behind and away from it.

Best for foreground architecture, portraits, vehicles, trees, sculptures, or objects that already dominate one side. Let a roof, shoulder, shadow, road, or horizon cross the seam.

Avoid centering the anchor or surrounding it with an equal paper border.

## Suspended scene aperture

Preserve one irregular photographic island inside a large quiet field. Let one source element—reflection, bridge, branch, waterline, path, or crowd rhythm—escape the island and continue as illustration.

Best for landscape scenes with a strong horizontal relationship or a compact cluster surrounded by atmosphere.

Keep the aperture between roughly one quarter and one half of the artwork. Avoid a perfect rectangle, generic torn snapshot, or multiple equal fragments.

Use this architecture selectively. Reject it when an edge-borne anchor, horizon exchange, depth passage, or reflective hinge can produce a less object-like and more continuous composition.

## Horizon exchange

Let the media change state along a real horizon, ridge, shoreline, facade, table edge, or other structural division. One side remains photographic around the hero while the other becomes a large printed or drawn field.

Best for city views, coastlines, open sky, interiors, roads, and layered landscapes.

Break the seam locally with one crossing form so the result is not a simple top/bottom split.

## Depth passage

Keep the nearest or farthest depth plane photographic and translate the other planes progressively. Use scale, reduced contrast, and material simplification to move through depth.

Best for streets, paths, interiors, mountain layers, forests, crowds, and scenes with strong perspective.

Do not apply a uniform fade. Each depth plane should change material for a reason.

## Reflective hinge

Use a reflection, shadow, glass surface, or mirrored repetition as the hinge between photography and illustration. One state remains factual; its counterpart becomes a simplified material translation.

Best for water, windows, polished floors, shadows, and repeated silhouettes.

Preserve the reflection axis and directional logic. Avoid a generic double exposure.

## Selection test

Choose the architecture that satisfies all three:

- the source already contains the required axis or depth relation;
- the photographic anchor remains unmistakable;
- the illustration field can occupy most of the remaining canvas without invented filler.

When two architectures fit equally well, prefer the one that needs less visible perimeter around the photographic anchor and allows more source forms to cross naturally between media.
