# Isometric Healing Block Sculpture Poster

Use the system `imagegen` skill and image-generation tool. Treat every uploaded photograph as an edit source and preserve a clear correspondence between its photographic and reconstructed states.

## Output contract

- Produce one independent raster poster for every uploaded photo. Never combine photos into a collage, contact sheet, diptych, or shared canvas.
- Use a 3:4 portrait canvas with one exact horizontal 50/50 division. Keep truthful photography in the upper half and the isometric modular sculpture in the lower half. Do not let either medium invade the other.
- The lower sculpture must preserve the source's identity through its distinctive mass, axes, curves, layering, occlusion, and negative space rather than through literal surface detail.
- Return every poster as a separate image. Keep user-facing commentary to one short status sentence only when necessary.

## Preserve the photographic upper half

Keep the source recognizably photographic. Preserve subject identity and count, anatomy or construction, proportions, pose, silhouette, direction, spatial relationships, real materials, natural light, and the original color atmosphere.

Apply only restrained art-magazine, independent-publication, or exhibition-photography color correction. Do not redraw, vectorize, beautify, materially relight, or apply block forms, paper texture, illustration marks, or graphic overlays to this region.

Crop deliberately for the upper half. When more area is needed, extend only plausible environmental background such as sky, ground, wall, water, foliage, or ambient space. Never stretch, warp, mirror, duplicate, or redesign the subject.

## Deconstruct before rebuilding

For each photograph, privately perform four decisions:

1. **Deconstruct:** identify the hero subject or inseparable relationship, its visual center of gravity, directional axes, major volumes, curves, overlaps, gaps, and depth order.
2. **Select:** retain only the structural cues that make this source immediately recognizable; remove incidental texture and minor detail.
3. **Distill:** translate those cues into a small vocabulary of rounded modular masses with clear size, height, depth, and spacing roles.
4. **Reconstruct:** arrange the masses in one coherent isometric space that preserves identity, posture, direction, narrative relationship, and meaningful negative space.

Also identify the source's most vital color relationship, overall temperature, value hierarchy, cool/warm exchange, and one key accent. Do not expose this analysis or repeat the user's design brief.

## Build a source-specific block sculpture

Use rounded, concise, tactile modules rather than a fixed brick system. Combine softened cuboids, cylinders, arches, slabs, wedges, capsules, curved bridges, nested shells, or other simple masses only when their geometry grows from the source.

Establish rhythm through scale contrast, high/low steps, foreground/background overlap, local suspension, nesting, crossing, density changes, and broad quiet space. Modules may join, stack, interlock, pass through, hover slightly, or shift out of alignment, but every move must clarify the hero's structure or energy.

Adapt the abstraction to the subject:

- people and animals: preserve characteristic pose, balance, direction, gesture, and relational distance without turning bodies into stiff mannequins;
- plants: preserve branching, growth, lean, bloom or foliage mass, and open gaps;
- architecture: preserve massing, openings, circulation, cantilevers, facade rhythm, and spatial voids;
- objects, food, and vehicles: preserve functional silhouette, key parts, orientation, and contact or movement;
- landscapes and natural subjects: preserve terrain, flow, layering, horizon, erosion, or weather as volumetric rhythms.

Do not mechanically disassemble the source, reproduce it brick by brick, force it onto a square grid, or reduce it to pixelated steps. The result should feel like a contemporary modular art sculpture with calm imagination—not a construction-kit diagram.

## Compose an airy isometric field

Choose the isometric viewpoint and layout from the hero's weight, posture, facing direction, and spatial relationship. Use one stable main axis, intentional front/back depth, and large areas of clean negative space. Avoid automatic centering and reusable podium compositions.

Keep the hero as the single focal system. If parts float, make their spacing and shadow visibly connected to the whole. Use soft contact and cast shadows to clarify stacking, suspension, and depth without creating a heavy CGI stage.

Present the finished lower artwork on a clean, light ground as a flat editorial poster. Do not add a display plinth, box packaging, showroom, desk, wall, hands, frame, or product mockup.

## Translate color from the photograph

Derive every lower-half color from the upper photograph. First identify its most recognizable and life-filled hue relationship, overall temperature, value levels, cool/warm echoes, and key accent. Then make that system softer, clearer, brighter, and airier while preserving the source's hue identity and emotional memory.

Use:

- one principal color occupying the largest masses;
- a few source-derived supporting colors for structural layers;
- lighter and darker variations from the same families to clarify depth;
- one very small, lively source-supported accent.

Raise lightness, reduce harsh contrast, and clean muddy mixtures without inventing a preset therapeutic palette. Different photographs must naturally produce different systems. Keep color rich but ordered, cute without sweetness, soft without weakness, and lively without noise.

Do not force pastel pink, blue, cream, or any other color absent from the source. Avoid fixed color cards, rainbow blocking, neon accents, sugary candy combinations, dull gray casts, and generalized Morandi desaturation.

## Use tactile matte materials

Give every module a delicate matte surface with soft diffuse reflection and subtle pulp, wood, powder-wax, chalk, or flexible-material tactility. Use rounded edges, restrained microtexture, gentle ambient occlusion, and soft shadows.

Avoid plastic specular highlights, metallic surfaces, glass, hard lacquer, heavy ray-traced CGI, excessive beveling, identical molded pieces, visible studs, branded bricks, and cheap toy texture.

## Integrate editorial microtype

Derive one concise 2–5 word title from the source's light, action, space, mood, or symbolic meaning. Add only a few supported state words, location terms, numbers, direction labels, or micro-notes. Do not use years.

Arrange text along module edges, gaps, the main visual axis, or negative space. It may align, tuck behind, or lightly overlap a mass when legibility remains clear. Typography should feel structurally integrated, airy, and precise—not like a caption pasted beneath a toy.

Do not invent logos, brands, unsupported locations or facts, long explanations, pseudo-language, signatures, or watermarks.

## Compile the image-edit prompt

Write one source-specific production prompt per input photo containing:

1. one independent 3:4 portrait output and the exact 50% photography / 50% block-sculpture split;
2. the upper-half preservation invariants, restrained color correction, deliberate crop, and any safe background extension;
3. the hero's selected identity cues, axes, major volumes, curves, overlaps, gaps, posture, and narrative relationship;
4. the exact modular vocabulary, isometric viewpoint, size and height hierarchy, interlocks, suspensions, depth order, and negative-space shape;
5. the source-derived principal, supporting, tonal, and accent colors with explicit area hierarchy;
6. the matte pulp, wood, wax, chalk, or soft-material tactility, rounded edges, diffuse light, and gentle shadows;
7. the exact 2–5 word title and optional microtext with their module-aware placement;
8. hard exclusions: no multi-photo layout, no altered or redrawn upper subject, no approximate split, no literal detailed replica, no LEGO or branded brick system, no pixelation, no mechanical exploded parts, no children's cartoon, no glossy plastic, no metal, no heavy CGI, no e-commerce toy display, no fixed palette, no year, no mockup environment, no logo, and no watermark.

Use concrete geometry, spacing, color, and material language rather than relying on adjectives such as “premium,” “cute,” “healing,” or “3D.”

## Inspect and correct

Review every result at full size and thumbnail size. Verify the exact split, truthful upper photography, immediate lower-subject recognition, coherent isometric depth, source-specific module geometry, intentional negative space, derived color identity, tactile matte surfaces, integrated microtype, and absence of toy-display cues.

If recognition is weak, restore the source's dominant axis, silhouette, key gap, or spatial relationship. If it resembles pixel art or LEGO, vary module geometry and remove grid logic, studs, and repeated bricks. If it looks like a glossy toy, flatten specular highlights and add subtle tactile surface variation. If color feels generic, re-anchor every hue to the upper image and delete unsupported additions. If it becomes cluttered, merge small parts into larger masses and restore quiet space. Regenerate with one targeted correction when needed.
