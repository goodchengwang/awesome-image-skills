# Philosophical Continuous Line Poster

Use the system `imagegen` skill and image-generation tool. Treat each uploaded photograph as the edit source. Distill its visible relationship into one quiet proposition, then draw only the lines and silences needed to hold that meaning.

## Output contract

- Produce one independent raster poster for every uploaded photo. Never combine photos into a collage, contact sheet, comparison layout, diptych, or shared canvas.
- Use a 3:4 portrait canvas with one exact horizontal 50/50 division. Keep truthful photography in the upper half and the philosophical line artwork in the lower half. Do not let either medium invade the other.
- Preserve immediate correspondence through the hero's pose, proportion, direction, relational distance, decisive contour, and one or two identity cues.
- Use warm white or paper white, fine black line, large silence, and zero or one tiny meaningful color accent.
- Use one short English title and one or two microtext groups only.
- Return every poster as a separate image. Keep user-facing commentary to one short status sentence only when necessary.

## Preserve the photographic upper half

Keep the source recognizably photographic. Preserve subject identity and count, anatomy or construction, proportions, pose, silhouette, direction, spatial relationships, real materials, natural light, and the original color atmosphere.

Apply only restrained art-magazine, independent-publication, or exhibition-photography color correction. Do not redraw, vectorize, beautify, materially relight, or apply line art, typography, color accents, or graphic overlays to this region.

Crop deliberately for the upper half. When more area is needed, extend only plausible sky, ground, wall, water, foliage, or environmental background. Never stretch, warp, mirror, duplicate, or redesign the subject.

## State the core visual proposition

Before drawing, privately identify:

1. the one hero subject or inseparable relationship carrying recognition;
2. the source's emotional tension, spatial relation, action direction, time state, or metaphor;
3. one plain proposition in the form `[subject or relationship] + [action/state] + [tension or implication]`;
4. the decisive pose, gap, touch, gaze, boundary, path, horizon, overlap, or contour that makes the proposition visible;
5. the minimum identity cues required for correspondence;
6. which source detail carries the strongest emotional color value, if any;
7. one extremely short English title and one or two supporting notes.

Do not use generic themes such as “freedom,” “hope,” “memory,” or “connection” unless the source relation visibly supports them. Do not expose this analysis or repeat the user's design brief.

## Reduce by subject type

Choose lines according to what the source actually is:

- people: preserve posture, gaze, gesture, touch, distance, and relation; omit descriptive anatomy and clothing detail;
- animals: preserve movement, weight, alertness, species-defining contour, and relation to ground or companion;
- plants: preserve growth direction, stem/branch rhythm, leaf or bloom mass, lean, and surrounding air;
- objects and vehicles: preserve functional silhouette, one structural turn, direction, support, and human or environmental relation;
- architecture and interiors: preserve proportion, boundary, opening, axis, enclosure, threshold, and spatial interval;
- landscape and nature: preserve horizon, terrain, waterline, flow, contour, distance, and one decisive event.

Use a few connected or closely related lines rather than a complete contour. Leave out surfaces, textures, repeated windows, foliage detail, facial detail, and background objects unless they are the proposition.

Avoid a one-line gimmick that sacrifices recognition, and avoid using the same face/profile/hand motif for unrelated sources.

## Draw with breath and incompletion

Use fine black continuous lines with controlled variation in pressure, speed, curvature, and pause. Let a line:

- begin after an implied contour has already started;
- stop before completing a form;
- connect two subjects or regions;
- miss, offset, cross, or overlap another line;
- extend into blank space as direction or duration;
- flatten into a horizon or rise into a gesture;
- leave a gap that represents distance, silence, or hesitation.

Keep the line fluid and natural, with subtle human variation but no jittery sketchiness. Use only a few short supporting strokes where identity demands them.

Do not outline everything, repeat contours, hatch shadows, fill black masses, add decorative curves, use smooth icon geometry, or leave arbitrary unfinished marks with no meaning.

## Compose one image, one relation, one silence

Use one core motif or inseparable relationship. The hero may be small, offset, partially cropped, suspended, or placed near an edge. Let its scale and position follow the proposition.

Shape large blank areas as active content:

- distance between subjects;
- time before arrival or after departure;
- loneliness around a small figure;
- intimacy compressed into a narrow gap;
- waiting in front of an open path;
- flow beyond a contour;
- incompletion held after a stopped line.

Define one center of gravity and one reading path. Use line spacing, approach, separation, crossing, pause, and direction to carry emotion.

Avoid mechanical centering, full-page linework, decorative balance, many motifs, complex scenery, floating ornaments, text blocks, and generic minimal-poster templates.

Present the result as a flat paper artwork. Do not add a frame, wall, desk, sketchbook, pen, gallery mockup, product card, or installation scene.

## Preserve zero or one color memory

Use a color accent only when one source detail carries unique narrative or emotional weight: a flower, light, garment fragment, object, eye, path mark, node, short line, or relationship focus.

Extract that hue from the upper photograph and gently purify it. Use the smallest area that remains visible—one point, tiny object, short segment, or compact node.

The accent must answer the proposition. If removing it does not change identity, relation, or emotional focus, omit it and keep the lower artwork black and white.

Avoid coloring the title, spreading accents, using more than one hue, filling a shape, adding a red dot by default, or treating color as decorative contrast.

## Write a quiet English annotation

Derive one extremely short English title from the source's emotion, relationship, action, time, distance, state, or metaphor. Do not directly name the object or place unless identity itself is the proposition.

Add one or two microtext groups: a status word, number, brief note, or one original restrained philosophical sentence. Do not attribute a quote unless the user supplied it. Do not invent dates, locations, measurements, or archive facts.

Make text share the line structure. It may follow line direction, stop at a line ending, occupy a deliberate silence, align to a contour, be crossed or divided by a line, or use one line as its baseline or connector.

Use fine weight, broad tracking, small caps, vertical arrangement, or asymmetric placement. Avoid commercial slogans, long copy, decorative type piles, centered headlines, logos, brands, signatures, pseudo-language, or watermarks.

## Compile the image-edit prompt

Write one source-specific production prompt per input photo containing:

1. one independent 3:4 portrait output and the exact 50% photography / 50% philosophical-line split;
2. the upper-half preservation invariants, restrained color correction, crop, and any safe background extension;
3. the core proposition, hero or relationship, tension, time state, metaphor, decisive pose/gap/touch/path/horizon, and minimum identity cues;
4. the exact few lines to retain, omitted contour sections, start/stop points, connections, offsets, crossings, extensions, and meaningful gaps;
5. the subject-specific reduction logic and all deleted descriptive detail;
6. the hero scale, offset/crop/suspension, center of gravity, reading path, and exact emotional role of each large blank region;
7. the zero-or-one source-derived accent hue, smallest placement, and identity/relationship/emotion function;
8. the exact short English title and one-to-two microtext groups, with factual support and their line-direction/end/gap/contour/crossing/baseline relation;
9. hard exclusions: no multi-photo layout, no altered or redrawn upper subject, no approximate split, no complete realistic contour, no detailed line drawing, no repeated sketch lines, no hatching, no decorative curves, no vector icon, no generic red dot, no more than one accent hue, no commercial layout, no false quote/data, no logo, and no watermark.

Use concrete proposition, line action, gap, scale, distance, and silence language rather than relying on adjectives such as “premium,” “philosophical,” “poetic,” or “meditative.”

## Inspect and correct

Review every result at full size and thumbnail size. Verify the exact split, truthful upper photography, recognizable distilled hero, one proposition, few meaningful lines, active blank space, zero or one exact color memory, one title, one-to-two microtext groups, and no decorative excess.

If it looks like a normal line drawing, delete most contours and retain only the proposition-bearing ones. If it becomes arbitrary abstraction, restore pose, relation, and identity cues. If blank space feels empty, assign it a specific distance, time, or emotional role and reposition the hero. If color feels decorative, remove it. If the philosophical text sounds generic, rewrite it from the visible source relation. Regenerate with one targeted correction when needed.
