# Abstract Memory Editorial Poster

Use the system `imagegen` skill and image-generation tool. Treat each uploaded photograph as the edit source. Let a few abstract forms preserve the visual memory while typography supplies order, rhythm, and close-reading detail.

## Output contract

- Produce one independent raster poster for every uploaded photo. Never combine photos into a collage, contact sheet, comparison layout, diptych, or shared canvas.
- Use a 3:4 portrait canvas with one exact horizontal 50/50 division. Keep truthful photography in the upper half and the abstract-memory editorial composition in the lower half. Do not let either medium invade the other.
- Preserve immediate correspondence through mass ratio, axes, curves, posture, overlap, occlusion, negative space, asymmetry, and a few decisive source details.
- Use a clean ivory lower ground, a strict limited palette, one 2–5 word English title, and two to four microtext groups.
- Return every poster as a separate image. Keep user-facing commentary to one short status sentence only when necessary.

## Preserve the photographic upper half

Keep the source recognizably photographic. Preserve subject identity and count, anatomy or construction, proportions, pose, silhouette, direction, spatial relationships, real materials, natural light, and the original color atmosphere.

Apply only restrained art-magazine, independent-publication, or exhibition-photography color correction. Do not redraw, vectorize, beautify, materially relight, or apply abstract shapes, typography, paper texture, or graphic overlays to this region.

Crop deliberately for the upper half. When more area is needed, extend only plausible environmental background such as sky, ground, wall, water, foliage, or ambient space. Never stretch, warp, mirror, duplicate, or redesign the subject.

## Deconstruct, select, distill, reconstruct

Before prompting, privately perform four passes:

1. **Deconstruct:** identify the hero subject or inseparable relationship, major masses, size ratios, main and secondary axes, curves, crossings, layers, occlusions, gaps, asymmetry, and spatial center of gravity.
2. **Select:** retain only the structural and narrative cues required for immediate correspondence; delete descriptive texture and incidental objects.
3. **Distill:** map each retained cue to a small number of geometric shapes, lines, cut planes, color fields, or shaped voids.
4. **Reconstruct:** arrange those forms as one balanced abstract memory with clear hierarchy, scale contrast, positive/negative shape, and quiet paper.

Also identify the source's light, space, action, material, and emotion; its most character-bearing color; one dark structural color; one light or neutral color; and any tiny accent that is truly essential.

Do not expose this analysis or repeat the user's design brief.

## Preserve memory through formal evidence

Use a limited vocabulary of circles, arcs, rectangles, wedges, ribbons, broad contours, thin axes, planes, and voids only when they map to source evidence.

Preserve:

- distinctive mass proportions and scale contrast;
- the hero's dominant axis, lean, direction, and center of gravity;
- characteristic curves, openings, notches, and silhouette turns;
- important crossing, layering, adjacency, and occlusion relationships;
- meaningful negative space between inseparable subjects or parts;
- asymmetry that carries posture, movement, or personality.

Merge small details into larger forms. Let one shape represent several related source parts when their shared mass is more memorable than individual description. Use one or two fine structural lines only when they restore identity or direction.

Do not reduce the hero to a generic icon, arrange equal shapes in a row, build a symbol library, use arbitrary abstract decoration, or preserve detail through miniature illustrations.

## Compose one abstract visual memory

Place the abstract hero according to its original weight, action, and relational space. It may be offset, cropped, stretched along an axis, partially nested, or allowed to cross a page edge. Keep one center of gravity and one dominant form.

Use scale difference, overlap, occlusion, density, shaped gaps, and large ivory blank areas to create hierarchy. Auxiliary forms must remain few and belong to the same source system.

Build a reading path with an entry, one focal hold, one smaller relational discovery, and a quiet exit. Avoid rigid symmetry, even distribution, many small fragments, decorative balance, centered logo composition, complex collage, and a reusable gallery-poster template.

Present the result as a flat editorial artwork. Do not add a frame, wall, desk, book mockup, gallery installation, product card, branding board, or presentation scene.

## Translate a strict source palette

Extract and simplify the upper photo into:

1. one principal color carrying the most identity and life;
2. one deep structural color for the visual skeleton;
3. one light or mid-value neutral supporting the ivory ground;
4. optionally, one tiny key accent when a source detail truly needs it.

Assign colors by area and structural role. Let the principal color dominate the memorable form, the deep color define axes or weight, the neutral mediate layers, and the accent mark one event.

Preserve the source's temperature and spirit. Keep color clean, restrained, and alive. Avoid equal color distribution, overall grayness, forced desaturation, fixed modernist palettes, unrelated beige, rainbow schemes, neon, candy color, muddy mixing, and multiple accents.

## Build an editorial microtype system

Create one poetic English main title of two to five words from the source's light, space, action, material, emotion, or metaphor. The title carries mood rather than merely naming the object.

Add two to four microtext groups chosen from supported information:

- a short phrase or state word;
- a place or object descriptor;
- a number, section, chapter, or sequence marker;
- a scale-like number or direction word;
- a material term, archival label, or micro-explanation.

Do not use years. Do not invent places, measurements, sequence facts, archive data, materials, or quotations. Abstract-looking numbers may be used only when they are clearly compositional labels rather than false claims.

Use a restrained serif family with a slender editorial sans serif. Establish hierarchy with one small-to-medium title, one extremely tiny group, and one or two intermediate groups. Vary size, weight, tracking, direction, and spacing deliberately.

## Make type and form share structure

Align text to geometric axes, edges, contours, cropped corners, or negative-space channels. It may run vertically, rotate, split into columns, open its tracking, hug an edge, press into a corner, cross a color field, nest between forms, or align through an occlusion.

Use type to extend a line, stop a movement, balance a mass, activate a blank region, or reveal scale. Every text group needs a compositional job and a clear reading order.

Avoid a conventional centered headline, equal text blocks, dense body copy, decorative pseudo-data, branding slogans, logos, signatures, malformed text, pseudo-language, or watermarks.

## Compile the image-edit prompt

Write one source-specific production prompt per input photo containing:

1. one independent 3:4 portrait output and the exact 50% photography / 50% abstract-memory split;
2. the upper-half preservation invariants, restrained color correction, crop, and any safe background extension;
3. the lower hero, relationship, mass ratios, axes, curves, crossings, layers, occlusions, negative spaces, asymmetry, and center of gravity;
4. the exact selected cues, deleted details, geometric mappings, merged forms, cut planes, structural lines, and shaped voids;
5. the dominant form, offset or crop, scale hierarchy, positive/negative balance, entry, focal hold, relational discovery, and quiet exit;
6. the clean ivory ground and exact principal, deep structural, neutral, and optional accent colors with area assignments;
7. the exact 2–5 word English title and its emotional source;
8. the exact two-to-four microtext groups, what each contains, and the factual support for any place, number, material, or label;
9. each text group's font role, size contrast, tracking, orientation, alignment, nesting, crossing, corner, or negative-space function;
10. hard exclusions: no multi-photo layout, no altered or redrawn upper subject, no approximate split, no arbitrary abstraction, no generic icon, no equal shape row, no many small fragments, no complex collage, no fixed palette, no overall grayness, no year, no false archival data, no conventional headline, no commercial template, no logo, and no watermark.

Use concrete source evidence, shape mapping, color area, hierarchy, and typographic function rather than relying on adjectives such as “premium,” “abstract,” “editorial,” or “collectible.”

## Inspect and correct

Review every result at full size and thumbnail size. Verify the exact split, truthful upper photography, immediate abstract correspondence, source-backed shapes, one dominant memory, clean ivory ground, strict color hierarchy, one 2–5 word title, two-to-four distinct microtext groups, and a clear reading path.

If the abstraction loses identity, restore mass ratio, main axis, decisive curve, and key negative space. If shapes feel arbitrary, delete anything without a source mapping. If the composition looks like icons, merge forms and reintroduce overlap, asymmetry, and scale contrast. If typography looks pasted on, align each group to a form, edge, axis, or void and give it a structural job. If microtext invents facts, replace it with supported state or material language. Regenerate with one targeted correction when needed.
